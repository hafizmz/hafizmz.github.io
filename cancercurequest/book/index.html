Mentari’s Mission

How the hospital crew finds and treats cancer

Written by Hafiz Zin
Draft edition, November 2026

Mentari the sun bear loves three things: climbing, honey, and Big Bear.

But lately her leg hurts when she climbs. Above her knee there is a bump that was not there before.

Mentari feels puzzled.

“Big Bear,” she says, “come and look at this.”

What do you do when something hurts and does not go away?
Curious corner
A bump or a pain that stays for many days should be shown to a grown-up and a doctor. Most bumps are not cancer, but it is always right to check.
2
3

At the hospital, Mentari feels worried. She holds Big Bear’s paw tight.

A doctor kneels down to say hello.

“I’m Dr. Sina. This is Dr. Intan, and this is our crew. We will find it. We will check it. We will treat it. Together.”

How many helpers can you count in the crew?
Curious corner
Dr. Sina is an oncologist, a cancer doctor. Dr. Intan is a paediatric oncologist, a doctor who looks after children with cancer. More than ten different jobs work together to care for one patient.
4
5

First, the crew must find out what is inside the bump.

Mentari rests her leg on the X-ray table. “Still as a sleeping bear,” says the radiographer.

Click! On the screen is a picture of her thigh bone. One part looks fuzzy.

Find it.

Can you find your own thigh bone? It is the longest bone in your body.
Curious corner
X-rays pass easily through skin and muscle, but bone stops them. That is why bones look white in the picture. An X-ray is often the first test for a sore bone.
6
7

Next comes the magnet camera. It is a long tunnel that goes knock, knock, knock!

Mentari wears headphones and listens to a story. Big Bear sits close by.

The picture shows a lump growing on her thigh bone.

Can you lie as still as Mentari while you count to five?
Curious corner
An MRI scanner uses a strong magnet and radio waves, and no X-rays. It shows soft parts, like muscle and the lump, more clearly than an X-ray can.
8
9

Two more cameras check the rest of her body.

The spinning camera takes a picture of her chest.

For the glow camera, the nurse says, “One small, quick pinch.” Mentari squeezes Big Bear’s paw and feels brave.

On the screen, the lump on her leg glows.

What helps you feel brave?
Curious corner
A CT scanner uses X-rays from many directions to draw slices of the body. A PET scanner follows a special sugar. Busy cells, like cancer cells, use more sugar, so they glow. The brain glows too, because it is always busy.
10
11

Now the crew must check the lump.

Mentari has sleepy medicine, so she feels nothing. Dr. Munir, the bone surgeon, takes a piece of the lump, as tiny as a grain of rice.

In the lab, the pathologist adds purple dye, then pink dye.

Check it.

Which colour went on first?
Curious corner
Taking a tiny sample is called a biopsy. It is the only way to be sure what a lump is. The purple dye sticks to the middle of each cell, called the nucleus. The pink dye colours the rest.
12
13

Under the microscope are lots of small, round cells, crowded together.

“Cells are tiny building blocks,” says Dr. Sina. “They make copies, and then they stop. These ones forgot to stop. That is called cancer.”

Mentari whispers, “Did I do something wrong?”

“No. It is nobody’s fault. And nobody can catch it from you.”

Can you spot the cells that keep on copying?
Curious corner
Mentari’s cancer is called Ewing sarcoma. It usually starts in a bone. Under the microscope it looks like many small, round cells packed closely together.
14
15

The whole crew sits around one big table. Mentari asks, “What happens next?”

“First, medicine to shrink the lump,” says Dr. Intan. “Then an operation, and radiotherapy. Then more medicine.”

“And I will look after your bone,” says Dr. Munir, the bone surgeon. “We will do it together.”

Whose tool is whose? Match each helper to a tool.
Curious corner
Dr. Munir is an orthopaedic surgeon, a doctor who operates on bones. The crew meets to choose the best plan and the best order for each patient.
16
17

“Where does my medicine come from?” asks Mentari. Dr. Sina shows her a research lab. There are no patients here, only cells in little dishes.

A scientist puts the same number of drops into every dish. One new medicine is too weak. One hurts the healthy cells too. One calms the confused cells and is gentle on the healthy ones.

“That one,” says the scientist, “is worth more tests.”

Which dish would you choose? Why?
Curious corner
This is research, not treatment. New medicines are tested on cells in a lab first, never on patients. Doctors can use a medicine in hospital only after many years of careful tests.
18
19

Medicine day. The nurse reads Mentari’s name band. “Right bear, right medicine!” Drip, drip, drip.

The medicine travels in her blood to find confused cells, wherever they are. Mentari feels tired, and her fur falls out for a while. She picks a cosy yellow beanie.

After many medicine days and rest weeks, the lump is smaller.

Treat it.

Which beanie would you choose for Mentari?
Curious corner
This medicine is called chemotherapy. Dr. Intan chooses it and checks how Mentari is doing. It works on cells that copy quickly. Hair cells copy quickly too, so hair may fall out for a while and then grow back. Rest weeks give healthy cells time to recover.
20
21

Operation day. The anaesthetist gives Mentari sleepy medicine through a soft mask. She sleeps and feels nothing.

Dr. Munir takes out the part of the bone with the lump, and puts in a strong new piece.

When Mentari wakes up, her leg is sore and wrapped up, and Big Bear is right there.

Who was there when Mentari woke up?
Curious corner
The surgeon takes a little healthy edge too, so no confused cells are left behind. A doctor called Ibnu Sina wrote about this idea 1,000 years ago. The new piece can be bone or metal, so the leg stays strong.
22
23

Next is radiotherapy. Dr. Fiz, the medical physicist, has checked the machine. It is safe and exact.

Mentari must be a statue again.

The big machine turns around her and sends invisible beams from different sides. They all meet at one spot on her leg. She cannot feel them at all.

“All done for today!” Big Bear gives her a big hug. She will come back for a little more each day.

Can you be a statue until I say “all done”?
Curious corner
Each beam is gentle on its own. Where the beams cross, they add up and stop the confused cells. Dr. Fiz plans the beams and tests the machine before it is used. The beams are only there while the machine is on, so hugs are safe.
24
25

Mentari has more medicine, to catch any confused cells left behind.

The physiotherapist helps her leg grow strong again. First with crutches. Then one step, two steps, three!

Mentari feels proud. Big Bear claps and claps.

How many steps can you count?
Curious corner
After an operation, muscles need practice to get strong again. A physiotherapist teaches the exercises. More chemotherapy after surgery is a normal part of the plan.
26
27

After many visits, Mentari feels much better. Her fur is growing back, soft and new.

She will come back for check-ups, and the crew will be here.

Mentari climbs to her favourite branch, slowly and carefully. Big Bear waves from below.

Find it. Check it. Treat it. Together.

Can you find the honeybee one last time? Who would you like to be in the crew?
Curious corner
Many children with cancer get better, and treatments keep improving because scientists keep asking questions. Maybe one day you will be one of them.

For grown-ups

Talking about cancer with young children

  • 1Use the real word. Say “cancer”, and explain it simply: some cells got confused and kept making copies.
  • 2Keep answers short and honest. It is fine to say “I don’t know. Let’s ask the doctor.”
  • 3Say these two things often. Cancer is not catching, and it is nobody’s fault.
  • 4Be truthful about what will be felt. Scans and radiotherapy cannot be felt. A needle is a small, quick pinch.
  • 5Let your child lead. Answer the question asked, then pause. Feelings often come out in play and drawing.
  • 6Keep routines and stay close. Being apart from you is often the biggest worry.
About getting better. Many children with cancer get better. How things go depends on the type of cancer and the care available, so ask your child’s care team about your own situation. This book explains; it does not give medical advice.

Who was Ibnu Sina?

Ibnu Sina was a doctor who lived about 1,000 years ago, from 980 to 1037. He wrote a huge book called The Canon of Medicine. Doctors learned from it for hundreds of years.
He wrote that a growing lump should be taken out early and completely. He also wrote rules for testing medicines. Our Dr. Sina is named after him.

Picture words

Cell
A tiny building block of your body.
Lump
Many cells bunched together.
Cancer
Cells that forgot how to stop copying.
Scan
A picture of the inside of you.
X-ray
Light we cannot see that passes through the body.
Bone
The hard frame that holds you up.
Magnet
It pulls and lines things up.
Microscope
It makes tiny things look big.
Dye
Colour that helps us see cells.
Surgery
Taking the lump out while you sleep.
Radiotherapy
Beams that meet at one spot.
Chemotherapy
Medicine that travels in the blood.

Try it at home

Echo game

Clap in a big room, then in a small room. Which one echoes? Sound pictures work the same way.

Statue game

Who can stay as still as a statue for 10 seconds? Staying still helps scans and radiotherapy work well.

Fair test

Grow two cups of bean sprouts the same way. Put one in the light and one in the dark. Change only one thing!
